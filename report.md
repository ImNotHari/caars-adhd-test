## 1. Introduction & Overview

The **Conners' Adult ADHD Rating Scales (CAARS)** is a standardized, norm-referenced psychological assessment tool specifically designed to evaluate Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) symptoms in adults. It was developed by **Dr. C. Keith Conners**, **Dr. Drew Erhardt**, and **Dr. Elizabeth Sparrow**, and is published by **Multi-Health Systems (MHS)**.

| Feature | Details |
|---|---|
| **Full Name** | Conners' Adult ADHD Rating Scales |
| **Abbreviation** | CAARS |
| **Developers** | C. Keith Conners, Drew Erhardt, Elizabeth Sparrow |
| **Publisher** | Multi-Health Systems (MHS) |
| **First Published** | 1999 (updated 2011; CAARS 2 released subsequently) |
| **Target Population** | Adults aged **18 years and older** |
| **Type** | Self-report and observer-report rating scale |
| **Reading Level** | 8th-grade reading level |

The CAARS was developed because ADHD was historically considered a childhood-only disorder. As research established that ADHD persists into adulthood in approximately **50–65% of cases**, there was a critical need for validated adult-specific assessment instruments. The CAARS filled this gap by providing a psychometrically robust, normed tool tailored to adult symptom presentation.

## 2. Purpose & Clinical Use

The CAARS serves several important clinical functions:

### Primary Purposes

- **Screening & Identification**: Quantify the presence, frequency, and severity of ADHD symptoms in adults.
- **Differential Diagnosis Support**: Help clinicians distinguish ADHD from other conditions with overlapping symptoms (e.g., anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder).
- **Baseline Profiling**: Establish a detailed symptom baseline before starting treatment.
- **Treatment Monitoring**: Track symptom changes over time to evaluate treatment effectiveness (pharmacological or behavioral).
- **Research Applications**: Provide standardized, quantifiable ADHD measures for clinical research studies.

### Important Note

> **The CAARS is NOT a standalone diagnostic instrument.** It is designed to be used as part of a comprehensive clinical evaluation that includes:
> - Structured clinical interview
> - Medical/developmental history review
> - Retrospective childhood symptom assessment (e.g., Wender Utah Rating Scale — WURS-61)
> - Cognitive/neuropsychological testing (when indicated)
> - Functional impairment assessment

## 3. Forms & Versions

The CAARS employs a **multi-informant approach**, meaning symptoms are assessed from two perspectives to provide a more complete clinical picture.

### Respondent Types

| Form | Abbreviation | Completed By |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-Report** | CAARS-S | The adult being evaluated |
| **Observer Report** | CAARS-O | A spouse, partner, family member, close friend, or colleague who knows the individual well |

### Length Variations

Each respondent type (Self-Report and Observer) is available in three lengths:

#### Long Version (CAARS-S:L / CAARS-O:L)
- **Number of Items**: 66
- **Administration Time**: ~10–15 minutes
- **Coverage**: The most comprehensive form — includes all 4 factor-derived subscales, 2 DSM symptom subscales, Total ADHD Symptoms score, the ADHD Index, and the Inconsistency Index.
- **Best For**: Comprehensive initial evaluations and detailed clinical assessments.

#### Short Version (CAARS-S:S / CAARS-O:S)
- **Number of Items**: 26
- **Administration Time**: ~5 minutes
- **Coverage**: Abbreviated versions (5 items each) of the 4 factor-derived subscales, plus the full 12-item ADHD Index and Inconsistency Index.
- **Best For**: Follow-up assessments, treatment monitoring, and situations where time is limited.

#### Screening Version (CAARS-S:SV / CAARS-O:SV)
- **Number of Items**: 30
- **Administration Time**: ~5 minutes
- **Coverage**: Focuses exclusively on core DSM diagnostic symptom criteria (Inattentive and Hyperactive-Impulsive symptoms) plus the 12-item ADHD Index.
- **Best For**: Initial screening, epidemiological studies, and quick identification of potential ADHD cases.

### Summary Table

| Version | Items | Self-Report | Observer | Key Scales Included |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Long** | 66 | CAARS-S:L | CAARS-O:L | All subscales, ADHD Index, Inconsistency Index |
| **Short** | 26 | CAARS-S:S | CAARS-O:S | Abbreviated factor scales, ADHD Index, Inconsistency Index |
| **Screening** | 30 | CAARS-S:SV | CAARS-O:SV | DSM symptom scales, ADHD Index |

## 4. Subscales & Factors Measured

The CAARS measures ADHD through three categories of scales:

### A. Factor-Derived Subscales (Empirically Derived)

These four subscales were derived through factor analysis of adult ADHD symptom patterns:

| Scale | Name | What It Measures | Example Behaviors |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Scale A** | Inattention / Memory Problems | Difficulty sustaining focus, poor organization, forgetfulness, distractibility | Trouble concentrating, losing track of tasks, forgetting appointments |
| **Scale B** | Hyperactivity / Restlessness | Physical fidgeting, inability to remain seated, internal sense of restlessness | Feeling driven by a motor, excessive movement, difficulty relaxing |
| **Scale C** | Impulsivity / Emotional Lability | Acting without thinking, low frustration tolerance, rapid mood swings | Quick temper, interrupting others, making impulsive decisions |
| **Scale D** | Problems with Self-Concept | Low self-esteem, poor self-confidence, negative self-perception | Feeling inadequate at work, low confidence in social situations |

### B. DSM Symptom Subscales (Criterion-Based)

These subscales directly map onto the DSM-IV/DSM-5 diagnostic criteria:

| Scale | Name | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Scale E** | DSM Inattentive Symptoms | 9 items aligned directly with DSM diagnostic criteria for the inattentive presentation |
| **Scale F** | DSM Hyperactive-Impulsive Symptoms | 9 items aligned directly with DSM diagnostic criteria for the hyperactive-impulsive presentation |
| **Scale G** | Total ADHD Symptoms | Combined composite of Scales E and F (all 18 DSM symptom items) |

### C. Validity & Index Scales

| Scale | Name | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Scale H** | ADHD Index | 12 items that most effectively discriminate adults clinically diagnosed with ADHD from non-ADHD controls. Acts as a single global indicator of ADHD risk. |
| **IncIdx** | Inconsistency Index | Evaluates pairs of conceptually similar items to detect careless, random, or erratic responding (validity check). |
| **NII** | Negative Impression Index *(CAARS 2 only)* | Detects symptom exaggeration, malingering, or overly negative self-presentation (validity check). |

## 5. How Scoring Works

### Step 1: Item-Level Rating

Each item on the CAARS is rated on a **4-point Likert scale**:

| Score | Label | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | Not at all, never | The behavior described is not characteristic at all |
| **1** | Just a little, once in a while | The behavior occurs occasionally or mildly |
| **2** | Pretty much, often | The behavior occurs frequently or moderately |
| **3** | Very much, very frequently | The behavior is pervasive, severe, or near-constant |

### Step 2: Raw Score Calculation

For each subscale, the individual item ratings are **summed** to produce a **raw score**.

**Example Calculation (Scale A — Inattention/Memory Problems):**

If Scale A contains 12 items and a respondent rates them as:
```
Item 1: 2, Item 2: 3, Item 3: 1, Item 4: 2, Item 5: 3,
Item 6: 2, Item 7: 1, Item 8: 3, Item 9: 2, Item 10: 2,
Item 11: 1, Item 12: 3
```
**Raw Score = 2 + 3 + 1 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 1 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 3 = 25**

### Step 3: Conversion to T-Scores

Raw scores are converted into standardized **T-Scores** using age- and gender-specific normative tables provided in the CAARS manual.

**T-Score Properties:**
- **Mean** = 50 (average score in the normative population)
- **Standard Deviation (SD)** = 10
- A T-Score of 60 means the individual scored **1 SD above** the average
- A T-Score of 70 means the individual scored **2 SD above** the average

### Step 4: Percentile Conversion

T-Scores correspond to **percentile ranks**, indicating where the individual falls relative to the normative sample.

### T-Score Interpretation Chart

| T-Score Range | Percentile Range | Classification | Clinical Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **< 60** | Below 84th | **Average / Unremarkable** | Within normal limits; no clinical concern |
| **60 – 64** | 84th – 92nd | **High Average / Borderline** | Slightly elevated; worth monitoring but not necessarily clinical |
| **65 – 69** | 93rd – 97th | **Elevated / Clinically Significant** | 1.5 SD above average; strongly suggests ADHD-related difficulties |
| **≥ 70** | 98th and above | **Very Elevated** | 2+ SD above average; indicates severe symptom impairment |

## 6. Types of Questions Asked

### Question Format

All CAARS items are **behavioral self-report statements** — short, clear descriptions of specific behaviors, thoughts, or feelings. The respondent rates how accurately each statement describes them (or the person being evaluated, in the case of the Observer Report) over the past month or in general.

Items are written at an **8th-grade reading level** to ensure broad accessibility.

## 7. Interpretation of Results

### Step-by-Step Interpretation Process

#### Step 1: Validity Check
Before interpreting any clinical scores, clinicians first evaluate the validity indicators:

- **Inconsistency Index**: If the score exceeds the validity threshold, the profile may reflect careless or random responding and cannot be reliably interpreted.
- **Negative Impression Index (CAARS 2)**: If elevated, the respondent may be exaggerating or feigning symptoms.
- **Missing Items**: Protocols with too many omitted items may be invalid.

#### Step 2: Review ADHD Index
The ADHD Index (Scale H) is examined as the first clinical indicator:
- **T ≥ 65**: Strongly suggests the individual's response pattern matches that of adults clinically diagnosed with ADHD.
- **T < 65**: Does not rule out ADHD but suggests the symptom profile is less consistent with typical ADHD presentations.

#### Step 3: Subscale Profile Analysis
The T-scores across all subscales are plotted to create a **symptom profile**:

| Profile Pattern | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Scale A elevated only | Predominantly **Inattentive** presentation |
| Scale B & F elevated | Predominantly **Hyperactive-Impulsive** presentation |
| Scales A, B, C, E, F all elevated | **Combined** presentation |
| Scale D elevated | Significant **self-esteem/self-concept** issues (may relate to ADHD or co-morbid depression) |
| Scale C elevated | Notable **emotional dysregulation** component |

#### Step 4: Multi-Informant Comparison
Comparing Self-Report (CAARS-S) with Observer Report (CAARS-O) reveals important clinical insights:

| Pattern | Clinical Implication |
|---|---|
| **Self > Observer** | Individual may be more distressed by internal symptoms (e.g., restlessness, executive dysfunction) than external observers notice |
| **Observer > Self** | Individual may lack awareness of their own behavioral impact on others |
| **Self ≈ Observer** | Good convergence between self-perception and external observation; strengthens diagnostic confidence |
| **Both Low** | ADHD less likely (though does not rule it out) |
| **Both High** | Strong evidence supporting ADHD diagnosis |

#### Step 5: Integration with Other Clinical Data
CAARS results are integrated with:
- Clinical interview findings
- Childhood symptom history (onset before age 12)
- Functional impairment evidence (work, relationships, daily life)
- Rule-out of other diagnoses

## 8. Psychometric Properties (Reliability & Validity)

The CAARS has been extensively validated across large normative and clinical samples.

### Reliability

| Metric | Self-Report (CAARS-S) | Observer Report (CAARS-O) |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal Consistency** (Cronbach's Alpha) | Median **alpha = 0.94** | Median **alpha = 0.95** |
| **Test-Retest Reliability** | Median **r = 0.92** | Median **r = 0.84** |
| **Inter-Rater Reliability** | **r = 0.44 – 0.54** (typical for multi-rater behavioral scales) | — |

**Internal Consistency**: Values above 0.90 are considered **excellent**, indicating that items within each subscale measure the same underlying construct cohesively.

**Test-Retest Reliability**: High values (greater than or equal to 0.80) demonstrate that scores remain stable over time when no true clinical change has occurred, confirming the instrument measures trait-like characteristics rather than temporary states.

**Inter-Rater Reliability**: The moderate correlation between self-report and observer ratings is expected and actually clinically informative — different observers see different aspects of behavior across contexts.

### Validity

| Validity Type | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Convergent Validity** | Strong correlations (**r = 0.48 – 0.83**) with other validated ADHD measures (e.g., Brown ADD Scales, WURS, ASRS) |
| **Discriminant Validity** | Effectively distinguishes ADHD from anxiety, depression, and other psychiatric conditions |
| **Criterion/Diagnostic Validity** | Overall correct classification rate of **84.7% – 92.5%** in distinguishing adults with ADHD from non-clinical controls |
| **Factor Structure** | Confirmed 4-factor solution (Inattention, Hyperactivity, Impulsivity/Emotional Lability, Self-Concept Problems) through confirmatory factor analysis |
| **Content Validity** | Items comprehensively cover DSM symptom criteria and associated functional impairments |

### Normative Data

The CAARS normative sample includes:
- Large representative community samples stratified by **age** and **gender**
- Separate norms for Self-Report and Observer Report
- Clinical comparison groups (adults with confirmed ADHD diagnoses)

## 10. CAARS 2 — Second Edition Updates

The CAARS 2 (Second Edition) represents a significant modernization:

### Key Changes from CAARS to CAARS 2

| Feature | CAARS (Original) | CAARS 2 |
|---|---|---|
| **DSM Alignment** | DSM-IV criteria | DSM-5-TR criteria |
| **Factor Structure** | 4-factor model | **5-factor model** (Inattention/Executive Dysfunction, Hyperactivity, Impulsivity, Emotional Lability, Problems with Self-Concept) |
| **Normative Data** | Original norms (1999) | **Updated, more diverse** representative normative samples |
| **Validity Scales** | Inconsistency Index only | Inconsistency Index **+ Negative Impression Index** (symptom exaggeration detection) |
| **Item Content** | Original items | **Modernized item language** reflecting contemporary adult life contexts |
| **Scoring** | Paper and software | Enhanced **digital scoring and reporting** capabilities |

### Why the Update Matters

1. **DSM-5 Alignment**: The original CAARS was based on DSM-IV criteria. DSM-5 made important changes to ADHD diagnostic criteria, including raising the age of onset from 7 to 12, reducing the symptom threshold for adults, and providing adult-specific examples.
2. **Emotional Lability Separation**: Emotional lability was combined with impulsivity in the original CAARS. The CAARS 2 separates these into distinct factors, recognizing emotional dysregulation as a distinct and clinically important feature of adult ADHD.
3. **Malingering Detection**: The addition of the Negative Impression Index helps clinicians identify individuals who may be exaggerating symptoms (e.g., for stimulant medication access).

## 11. Limitations & Considerations

| Limitation | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Not Standalone** | Cannot be used as the sole basis for ADHD diagnosis; must be part of a comprehensive evaluation |
| **Self-Report Bias** | Respondents may over-report (seeking diagnosis/medication) or under-report (denial/lack of insight) symptoms |
| **Cultural Factors** | Norms may not generalize equally across all cultural groups; some behaviors may be interpreted differently across cultures |
| **Comorbidity Overlap** | Symptoms of anxiety, depression, trauma, and other conditions can elevate CAARS scores without ADHD being present |
| **No Cognitive Measure** | The CAARS is a behavioral rating scale, not a cognitive/neuropsychological test — it does not directly measure attention or executive function performance |
| **Commercial Cost** | The instrument is copyrighted and requires purchase, which may limit accessibility in some clinical settings |
| **Observer Availability** | Obtaining a reliable observer report can be challenging for adults who live alone or have limited social connections |

## 12. Summary

The **Conners' Adult ADHD Rating Scales (CAARS)** is one of the most widely used, well-validated, and clinically respected instruments for evaluating ADHD symptoms in adults. Its key strengths include:

- **Multi-informant design** (Self + Observer reports) providing a balanced clinical picture
- **Multiple form lengths** (Long, Short, Screening) for flexible use across clinical contexts
- **Excellent psychometric properties** (internal consistency alpha >= 0.94, test-retest r >= 0.84)
- **Standardized T-score interpretation** with clear clinical cutoffs (T >= 65 = clinically significant)
- **DSM-aligned symptom subscales** enabling direct mapping to diagnostic criteria
- **Built-in validity checks** (Inconsistency Index, Negative Impression Index) to detect invalid protocols

The CAARS provides a structured, evidence-based framework for quantifying adult ADHD symptoms that, when used alongside clinical interviews and other assessments, significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy and treatment planning.
